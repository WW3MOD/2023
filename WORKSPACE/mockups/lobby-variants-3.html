Round three — DEFCON is the prelude, the ladder is the war

Round two drew yields onto DEFCON rows. That was the mistake, and every tab here fixes it the same way: two phases, drawn apart — a DEFCON posture phase that carries no warheads at all, a release gate, then a nuclear escalation phase whose steps advance on their own rule. A–C are the three you said were onto something, rebuilt in that vocabulary. D–F are new and deliberately push past what a lobby normally does.

One finding first, because it changes what is actually being asked for. The shipped code already separates them exactly as described. DefconEscalation runs DEFCON 3 → 2 → 1 and grants nothing; NuclearReleaseLadder refuses every yield — "until it has opened, every yield is refused" — until the match has been at DEFCON 1 for NuclearReleaseDelayTicks, which defaults to 10000 ticks = 10:00. So the prelude and the war are already two mechanisms. Two things are genuinely missing, and they are the whole of round three: the steps advance on detonations, not on a clock (each detonation doubles a shared pressure value; the rung index is the detonation count), and reaching a step grants nothing. Your arithmetic, on screen

  • Five yield steps already exist in code — 1 kt, 20 kt, 50 kt, 100 kt, game-enders. Opening at 1 kt when the gate lifts leaves four advances, so at 10 minutes each the enders arrive 40 minutes after DEFCON 1. That is your number, and it falls out of the shipped rung list without inventing anything.
  • The strip at the top exists so a host can see that number without doing the sum. With the default pace and one early kill it reads ≈ 47:30 end to end.

What changes underneath

  • new Steps advance on a rule you pick. Today the only thing that climbs the ladder is a detonation, so a match where nobody fires never escalates at all. A clock makes escalation happen to the players rather than because of them.
  • new Each step pays out. One warhead per side per step, held until used.
  • cut Dead Hand as a mode, and cut Sandbox as a game mode value — it collides with the sandbox roster and its Start-At-2 behaviour freezes autonomous fire for a whole match.

What I would question

  • A per-step editor with add/delete/reorder is a lot of lobby. It is also the only shape that lets a host build a ladder that skips 20 kt, or pays two warheads at the top and one below. B trades that away; C keeps it and spends the space differently.
  • "Held until used" means both sides can sit on a game-ender indefinitely. Real, and also means the match can end three seconds after step 5 at the whim of whoever is losing.

The idea

  • Four answers generate the whole thing: how far, how often, how much, and how long the prelude runs. The table below them is read-only — it exists so the four are never a guess.
  • new The timeline is absolute, not relative. Every row is a clock time from match start. "Nuclear from 12:30" is a sentence a host can plan an evening around; "Standard pace, 100 kt ceiling" is not.
  • The first-kill row is an estimate and drawn as one — DEFCON 2 ends on an event nobody can schedule. Everything below it shifts with it, which is honest and worth showing.

Trade

  • You lose per-step authoring entirely. No skipped yields, no uneven payouts.
  • You gain the only version here a stranger can read in five seconds, and a preset that is four values rather than a table.

Worth deciding either way

  • Should the ceiling being below the enders mean the match has no ending clock at all (drawn here), or should the enders still arrive on schedule and simply be the only thing the ladder ever hands out above the cap? The first is cleaner; the second is the only way "cap the yields but still end the match" is expressible.

The idea

  • Two widgets of visibly different size and colour, with a gate between them, say "these are not the same system" without a word of explanation. Round two's single list said the opposite by drawing them as one column.
  • The ladder reads bottom-up. It is the only orientation where the cap is at the top of the screen and the war climbs toward it, which is the mental picture the mode is named after.
  • Per-step authoring survives — including the thing B cannot express: a disabled middle step, so a host can build a ladder that jumps from tactical straight to strategic with nothing in between.

Trade

  • Everything is click-to-edit, so nothing tells you what a control does until you touch it. That is fine for the tenth match and hostile for the first — it probably needs B's generated summary line underneath as a permanent caption.
  • It is tall. On a 10-slot lobby this is competing with the player list for vertical space.

Why this is worth testing

  • Every other tab makes a host set rules and then work out the consequences. This one makes them set the consequences directly. "First nuke around sixteen minutes, done by forty-five" is what someone actually wants; pace and delay are just the arithmetic that gets there.
  • It is the only layout where the shape of the match is visible at a glance — a long conventional opening with a sudden ladder looks different from a slow burn, and you can see which you built before you play it.
  • Presets stop being value bundles and become shapes. "Cold War" is a wide blue band and a narrow amber one; "Armageddon" is the reverse. A host recognises the picture.

Where it breaks

  • Event-driven pacing has no honest position on a time axis. The first-kill rule is drawn as a tickbox that makes the strip approximate, which is the best I have — but it means the two pacing philosophies do not coexist comfortably here.
  • Dragging is a real UI, not a dropdown. This is the most expensive tab to build by a wide margin, and it wants a keyboard path for anyone who cannot drag precisely.
  • It quietly asserts matches have a length. Today they end when someone wins.

Why this is worth testing

  • It answers the one question the ladder view never does: how many nuclear weapons are in this match? Both sides can count them before the first shot, which is the entire strategic content of your payout model.
  • The ceiling, the step count and the "buyable" toggle all collapse into the sheet. A yield with zero free and zero buyable is simply absent — one mechanism instead of three.
  • It makes asymmetry expressible for free. A second column per side and you have a handicap match, or a scenario where one side opens with tactical superiority.

Where it breaks

  • It inverts cause and effect. Escalation stops being something the war produces and becomes an inventory that happens to unlock on a timer — which may be exactly the wrong feeling for a mode whose whole subject is losing control.
  • Per-yield buyability is a lot of expressive power nobody asked for, and it is the kind of control that produces unplayable lobbies.
  • "Spread the unlocks over 40 minutes" is a worse handle than "a step every 10 minutes" the moment a row is deleted.

Why this is worth testing

  • Everything else on offer assumes the host wants to design a match. Most do not — they want a match that is already a good one, and the two things they actually have an opinion about are how nuclear it gets and how long it takes.
  • Named cells are teachable. "We played Flashpoint last night" is a sentence; "Escalation, DEFCON 2, Fast, 100 kt" is not.
  • The full panel still exists, one click down, and it is any of A / B / C. This tab is a front for one of them rather than a rival to them — which is the honest reading of your "presets set the defaults, then show what changed."

Where it breaks

  • Sixteen names is sixteen things to balance and sixteen things to explain. Half of them will be played once.
  • The grid asserts the two axes are independent. "Instant + conventional" is a coherent game; "instant + enders" is a fifteen-minute match that is over before anyone builds anything, and the grid gives it equal billing with the good cells.
  • It hides the ladder entirely, and the ladder is the thing the mode is about. A host who never opens the panel never learns the system exists.
