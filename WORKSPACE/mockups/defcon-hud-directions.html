What the player sees, and how they learn the rules without being told them

Three directions for the in-game readout, drawn at true size against a stand-in battlefield. The one thing every direction has to do is teach the difference between the two transitions: DEFCON 3→2 is a clock and 2→1 is an event. Drawn the same way, the player will sit waiting for a bar that never fills.

Direction A — the strip · compact, docked, always present

at DEFCON 3 — the clock is running

DEFCON 3Positioning 2:41 to DEFCON 2
The border is closed. Neither side may cross it.

at DEFCON 2 — no clock, a trigger instead

DEFCON 2Weapons free —
Your units will not fire on their own. Every shot is one you order.
FIRST KILL ENDS THIS PHASE

Why the clock field goes to an em dash at DEFCON 2 instead of disappearing: the player has just spent minutes watching a countdown in that exact spot, so the emptied slot is what tells them this rung does not work like the last one. The pulsing trigger line then says what does end it.

Shows where you are going, not only where you are — a first-time player can read the whole shape of the match in one glance. Costs more screen, and repeats information that stays constant for most of a match.

Direction C — ambient · the screen itself carries the level

DEFCON 2

A thin edge-glow in the level's colour and nothing else persistent. Least disruptive by a distance, and it reads at any zoom — but it can carry the level and not the rule, so it only works if the transitions do all the teaching. Probably a companion to A rather than a rival to it.

The transition — the moment that actually teaches the system

DEFCON 1
A life has been taken. Autonomous fire is released.
held 4 s · then the readout returns

Whatever direction wins, this is where the player finds out what happened and why. Naming the cause in the second line — a life taken, the clock run out — is what turns a state change into a rule the player can infer without ever being told it.

The nuclear rung — a second, separate readout

shared between both players; appears only when nuclear release is on

Nuclear release20 kt
HOLD1kt20kt 100kt200kt+
Both sides are released to the same yield. Each use raises it.

Deliberately a different shape and colour from the DEFCON strip, because it is a different ladder that moves for different reasons. The red final step is the host's ceiling, drawn as a wall the ladder climbs toward — so its meaning is visible long before it is reached.

Wording — the part that has to survive a player who never reads a manual

LevelNameLine the player reads
DEFCON 3Positioning The border is closed. Neither side may cross it.
DEFCON 2Weapons free Your units will not fire on their own. Every shot is one you order.
DEFCON 1Open war Everything is released. Units engage on sight.

These three lines do more work than the graphics do. Each states a rule as what the player can and cannot do right now, never as the mechanic behind it — no stances, no autonomous targeting, no counters, no terrain. If the wording is wrong the readout fails however good it looks.
