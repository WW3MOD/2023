Escalation as an exchange — you arm your enemy by firing

The rule that makes this different from every ladder so far: a launch grants nothing to the launcher. It grants the next yield up to the people you fired at. So the ladder only climbs when both sides keep answering — and the only way to stop it is to take a hit and not answer in kind.

One thing to decide before anything else, because the mechanic turns on it. If firing strictly helps your enemy and never you, then between two good players the correct move is never fire — and the whole nuclear layer becomes scenery. Your own framing already says this ("you only do it if you are behind and you really need it"), so the mode needs something that makes falling behind final. A match clock does it: when the clock runs out the higher score wins, so the player who is behind must either fire or lose. The clock is what starts the spiral, and without it A and D below are the only variants that can ever reach the ending. Today's DoomsdayStrike already carries that clock — and note its current line reads "the winner is resolved from the FROZEN score", which is exactly the sentence your "everyone lost" ending overturns.

The whole rule set
· At 30:00 every player is granted one 1 kt warhead. Nothing before that.
· Firing grants the enemy the next yield up — and everything below it they do not already hold. You are granted nothing by your own launch. new
· So a warhead you fire is gone. Only being fired at refills you.
· Take a hit and do not answer in kind, and the ladder stops where it is. That is the only brake.
· A game-ender ends the match: 15 s for everyone to pick targets, then everyone is Lost.

Why it works, in one line

  • Firing helps you now and hurts you later, and the size of the later cost is chosen by your victim. That is a real decision under uncertainty and it needs no balance numbers to be tense.
  • The moral is not written on a screen anywhere — it is the payoff matrix. A player who escalates to win discovers they have handed their opponent the means to make winning impossible.

Three adjustments I would make

  • changed Add the match clock (see the box above). Without it, good players simply never fire.
  • changed Say what "the enemy" means with more than two players. A launch should arm every player you are hostile to — so in 3v3 one hothead arms three enemies, and in a free-for-all one launch arms five. That is a wonderful thing to have in the game, and an awful thing to leave undefined.
  • changed Retaliating "in kind" must be defined by yield, not by intent. Answering a 20 kt with a 20 kt is in kind and escalates nothing; answering with a 50 kt escalates. Otherwise nobody can tell which move they just made.

Where it can go wrong

  • The losing player can always deny the win by climbing to the top — "if I can't win, nobody does". That is exactly the doctrine, and it is also the thing your competitive players will complain about first. E is the answer to it if it bites.
  • Grants stack ("as well as all previous"), so the second exchange is bigger than the first. The spiral accelerates, which is right thematically and means the endgame arrives faster than the 30-minute setting suggests.

The one added line
· Every interval in which nobody launches, the ceiling drops one level.
· Warheads already granted are never taken away — de-escalation lowers what can be unlocked next, not what you hold. A weapon in the silo is a permanent consequence.

What it adds

  • A tug-of-war instead of a ratchet. A war can cool down and heat up twice in one match, which is a shape no version of this has had yet.
  • It makes a cease-fire a strategy: both sides stop, the ceiling falls, and the side that breaks the silence is visibly the one that did it.
  • The held-warheads exception is what keeps it from being a reset button — the arsenal is the scar the war leaves behind even after everyone calms down.

Against it

  • It softens the moral. "There will be no winners" becomes "there need be no winners, unless somebody keeps at it" — which is a different and gentler story.
  • Two clocks running at once (escalation and cooling) is the first thing here that is genuinely harder to explain than it is to play.

How it differs
· Being struck grants you the same yield — never a bigger one.
· ESCALATE is a separate order. It raises the ceiling for everyone, costs you a warhead, and is announced with the player's name. new
· There is no way up the ladder that is not somebody deliberately choosing it.

What it adds

  • Guilt has an author. In A the spiral is nobody's fault in particular. Here every step up has a name attached and a banner announcing it, which is much closer to the "poetic" register — the game remembers who crossed each line.
  • It separates using nuclear weapons from escalating, which is the actual distinction the doctrine turns on and the one every other version here blurs.
  • Proportional retaliation being free means the nuclear layer is genuinely used in every match, not just in lost ones — which fixes A's "good players never fire" problem without a clock.

Against it

  • An ESCALATE button is a strange object in an RTS and will need real UI work to not feel like a menu item. It is the most expensive variant here.
  • Free proportional retaliation means the map is being nuked constantly at the same yield, which could get numbing — the opposite failure to A's.

Why this one might be the best of them

  • One number on screen that only goes down, and everyone can move it. No unlock bookkeeping, no per-player state, nothing to explain in a tooltip — and the moral is more legible than in A, not less, because you watch the cost of every launch land on a shared dial.
  • It solves A's problem without a match clock: firing is always available, so the threat is real from minute one, and the price is always visible.
  • It is the cheapest of the five to build by a wide margin. The clock and the end-of-match salvo already exist in DoomsdayStrike — this wires detonations to the clock and changes who is called the winner.

Against it

  • It loses your exchange entirely. Nobody arms anybody; there is no gift, no retaliation, no reading of your enemy's intent. That is the most interesting thing in your version and this variant does not have it.
  • "Everyone holds a game-ender from minute one" is a very different match. Somebody will fire one at 0:30 to see what happens.

What changes

· A row for the map itself, so the total damage is a number somebody has to look at.

· If it ended without a game-ender, the winner is a winner as normal. Restraint is the only path to the word.

Why it is worth doing whichever rule wins

  • This is where the poetry actually lives. The rules make the spiral happen; the scoreboard is what tells the player what it meant, and it is the one screen everybody reads at the end of every match.
  • "Russia held more ground at the end. It is not called winning." does more moral work than any mechanic on the other four tabs, and it is a label and a string.
  • Keeping the normal win for a match that ended conventionally is what stops it being nihilism — the word winner still exists, and the way to earn it is to not reach for the thing.

Against it

  • Competitive players will want their win recorded. A ladder or a stats page has to decide what a nuclear draw counts as, and "everyone lost" is not a result a rating system can take.
  • Percentages of "standing" need a definition — buildings? value? population? — and whichever is picked, somebody will play to the metric rather than to the war.
