Every rung hands each side a warhead. The top rung ends the world.

Escalation stops being a permission system and becomes an economy: reaching a rung gives both sides one free warhead of that type, to use whenever they like. Buying more is a setting, off by default. The last rung is the game-ender, and using one ends the match — which is the Dead Hand, with no separate mode.

What this is

  • new Rungs pay out. Reaching a rung grants each side one warhead of the type it unlocked, held until they choose to use it. Escalation becomes something you receive, not merely something you are permitted.
  • new Buying is a setting, off by default — so a default match has exactly as many warheads as the ladder handed out, and both sides know the count.
  • cut Dead Hand as a mode. The top rung is the game-ender; firing one ends the match with the score frozen at launch. Same ending, arrived at by play.
  • new The reply window. Anyone still alive may fire their own game-ender at a target of choice before the match closes. Deciding to end it is not the same as deciding who wins.
  • new Countdown preset — your no-ladder case: no escalation, game-enders unlock on a timer, anyone may fire, reply window applies.

What I would question

  • A per-rung editor is a lot of lobby for one feature. B and C are two ways to keep the model and shrink the control.
  • "+1 each side, held indefinitely" means both sides may sit on a game-ender for the rest of the match. That is a real strategic object — but the match can also end three seconds after the final rung, at the whim of whoever is losing.

The idea

  • Three dropdowns produce the whole ladder. The ceiling decides how many rungs exist; the pace decides when they arrive; the payout decides what each is worth.
  • new The generated ladder is shown, greyed. A host sees exactly what their three choices produced — the editor from A becomes a preview.
  • "Escalates: only when someone dies" gives you event-driven pacing without a per-rung editor, and "both" is today's behaviour.

Trade

  • You lose per-rung authoring — no ladder that skips 20 kt, or pays 2 warheads at the top and 1 below.
  • You gain a lobby a stranger can read, and a preset that is genuinely three values rather than a table.

The ladder is not a setting. It is the picture you configure.

One widget: the ladder itself, drawn as it will appear in game. Click a rung to turn it on or off, click its payout to change it, drag the red line to set the ceiling. Nothing else on screen.

The idea

  • The thing being configured and the thing shown in game are the same picture. A host builds the ladder they will later watch climb.
  • Rungs are removable, so "1 kt is pointless on this map" is one click — the per-rung control of A without a table of dropdowns.
  • new Presets become shapes of ladder: Skirmish is a ladder with one rung, Armageddon is five, Countdown is two — conventional, then the enders on a timer.

Trade

  • It is a custom widget, not a list of dropdowns: the most build effort of anything here, and the hardest to keep readable at small window sizes.
  • Everything non-nuclear is behind a fold, which is either the point or a problem.

The match is one sentence. Change the words.

The lobby's job is to answer "what game are we about to play". So write that answer, and make the parts you can change into slots. No sections, no headings, no scrolling.

This match

A nuclear war on River Zeta, escalating every 4 minutes up to the game-enders, where each step hands both sides one warhead and extra warheads cannot be bought.
It ends when someone fires a game-ender, and everyone else gets 60 seconds to reply.

The idea

  • Every slot is a dropdown. The sentence is generated from the same values as any other layout — it is a different presentation, not different rules.
  • new The sentence rewrites itself: pick "conventional" and the nuclear clauses vanish, leaving "A conventional war on River Zeta. It ends when one side can no longer be relieved." An impossible combination cannot be written — the broken Doomsday default could not have existed here.
  • It is also the match summary. The same string can head the loading screen and the score screen.

Trade

  • Genuinely unusual, which cuts both ways: nobody has to learn a panel, but nobody has seen a lobby like it either.
  • Long option names get awkward mid-sentence, and localisation is harder — word order changes per language.

Both sides sign what they are and are not allowed to do.

The fiction is arms control, so make the lobby a treaty. Clauses are on or off, and what the treaty does not forbid is permitted. Escalation is the clause that expires.

Treaty of the match
Article I — No warhead above 100 kt may be used.
Article II — Release is staged: each 4 minutes unlocks the next yield and issues one warhead to each signatory.
Article III — Signatories may purchase further warheads beyond those issued.
Article IV — Crossing the demarcation line is forbidden while the treaty holds.
Article V — Use of a game-ender voids the treaty; all signatories may respond in kind within 60 s, then hostilities cease.
Cold War Article III struck
Strike every article and you have a skirmish.

The idea

  • Each rule is stated as what players may and may not do — the same discipline the in-game readout already follows, applied one layer earlier.
  • new Presets are named treaties, and striking articles is visibly a deviation from one: the delta chips write themselves.
  • Article IV is the DEFCON 3 border, which currently has no lobby presence at all despite being the most visible rule in the game.

Trade

  • Flavour on top of settings can get in the way when a host just wants to change starting cash — the fine print still needs an ordinary panel underneath.
  • Reads beautifully for two sides; needs care with more than two.
